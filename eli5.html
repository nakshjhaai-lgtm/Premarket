This site, explained like you are five

Simple words first. Every blue word links to the glossary, which has the exact grown up definition.

What is this website?

Every morning at 9 o'clock, before the Indian stock market wakes up, this website writes a short report. The report says: here is how the market will probably start today. Up, down, or flat. And here is how sure we are.

Think of it like a weather report, but for the stock market instead of the sky.

What is the NIFTY 50?

Imagine the 50 biggest shops in one big market. Someone adds up how all 50 shops are doing every day and turns it into one score. That score is called the NIFTY 50. When people say the market went up today, they usually mean this score went up.

What happens before the market opens?

The market sleeps at night. It wakes at 9:15 in the morning. But before it wakes, there are already clues about whether it will start in a good mood or a bad mood. This website collects those clues at 9:00, fifteen minutes before the bell.

There is even a warm up round from 9:00 to 9:15 called the pre open session, where everyone places their first orders without trading yet. It is like kids lining up before the school gate opens.

The clues we check

Clue 1: the practice price. While India sleeps, a copy of the NIFTY is already being traded far away, in a place called GIFT City in Gujarat and on exchanges abroad. That price is called GIFT Nifty. It is like a practice vote about where the market will open. It is our most important clue.

Clue 2: how the world slept. While India slept, America's markets finished their day and Japan and Hong Kong were already trading. If they had a bad night, India often starts grumpy too. We also check the price of oil (India buys a lot of it), gold, and a few other things. These are the market context. They set the mood. They do not give orders.

Clue 3: the fear thermometer. It is called India VIX. It does not say up or down. It says how bumpy the road is expected to be today. A high number means hold on tight, in either direction.

Clue 4: counting hands. After the market opens, we count how many stocks went up (hands raised) and how many went down (hands down). If the market goes up but most hands are down, something is fishy. This clue comes a little later, at 9:20, in the post open check.

Sometimes the clues disagree. Then we say so, out loud: conflicting signals, no clear answer today. Guessing anyway would be lying to you.

The computer that guesses the price

Alongside the clue reading, a computer program guesses tomorrow's NIFTY number. Actually there are two.

The student. One program, called an LSTM, studied almost 20 years of old NIFTY numbers, the way a student studies old exam papers. It learned patterns in how the numbers move one day after the next.

The well read outsider. The other program, TimesFM, was trained by Google on about a hundred billion numbers from all kinds of things, and it has never studied our NIFTY homework. It just looks at our recent numbers and gives an opinion. That is fair, because it cannot cheat.

The blend. We ask both, then combine their answers into one number called the ensemble. How much do we trust each one? We measured. Over the last year of guessing one day ahead, the outsider was right far more often, so it gets almost all the say, and the student gets a small say. The exact share is a number called w, and it is set by the measurements, not by us.

How the computer learns from its mistakes

Every morning the website writes down its guess in a notebook called the scorecard. The next day, when the real answer is out, it checks: how wrong were we? Each model gets its mistake written down, like marks on a report card. After enough days, the share of trust between the two guessers is recalculated from the recent marks. If the student starts doing better, its say grows. All by itself, every day.

The important part: we show you the marks. Every accuracy number on this site was measured on days the models had never seen while learning. Nobody here can see the future. We are just very honest about the odds.

The page with 50 stocks

There is also a page, Stocks, where the well read outsider program guesses the next few days for 50 famous companies: Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Toyota, an Indian bank or two, and more. Each guess comes with its own little track record, so you can see how often that guessing worked for that stock lately.

What this site cannot do

Its number guessing does not read the news. That is why, every morning at 08:40, the site also collects real headlines for every stock, checks who reports earnings this week, and flags big words like war, election, lawsuit, recall or rate cut. It shows these next to the numbers instead of hiding them inside the math, so you can weigh what the equations cannot see.

It cannot promise. Next day direction is genuinely hard: out of sample, honest models land only a little above half right, and stock prices are noisy. Anyone who promises you certain market predictions is not telling the truth.

It is not advice. It is a measurement instrument: it reads clues and shows its own track record. What you do with it is up to you.
